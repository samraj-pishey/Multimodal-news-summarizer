The big idea

This is the opening. In one breath you tell the jury what the project does: it reads a news article, the video that comes with it, and what people say in that video, and it produces a short summary plus a few key pictures.

Words to know

  • Multimodal: using more than one type of input. Here: text, images (video frames) and audio (speech).
  • Summarization: making a long text short while keeping the important points.
  • Heterogeneous sources: sources of different kinds: an article, a video and speech are very different types of data.
  • NLP (Natural Language Processing): the field of teaching computers to work with human language.
  • Framework: a complete pipeline of connected parts (data preparation, models, evaluation and an app), not a single model.
  • Automated summary generation: the computer produces the summary by itself, either by picking sentences (extractive) or by writing new ones (abstractive).

The big idea

There are two ways to summarize. Extractive picks existing sentences, like a highlighter. Abstractive writes new sentences, like a person writing notes. Most of our work is extractive, because both research papers we follow are extractive. At the end we added an abstractive model (BART) for comparison.

Words to know

  • Extractive summarization: choosing whole sentences from the original text. Safe: it can't invent facts, because every sentence is real.
  • Abstractive summarization: generating new sentences. Reads naturally, but can 'hallucinate', meaning it can state things the source never said.
  • ROUGE: the standard score for summaries. It counts how many words our summary shares with a human-written summary. Explained on slide 16.

What to say

There are two ways to summarize. Extractive is like a highlighter pen: we pick the best sentences that already exist, so nothing can be invented. Abstractive is like writing your own notes: more natural, but it can make mistakes. Most of our project is extractive, and we added an abstractive model at the end to compare.

If the jury asks

  • Which is better? It depends. Extractive is safer and easier to check. Abstractive is shorter and more readable. In our app we show both, side by side.
  • What is hallucination? When a model writes something that sounds right but isn't in the source. Extractive methods can't hallucinate because they only copy real sentences.

The big idea

This slide lists the nine steps that turn raw files into something a model can use. The most important ones: removing broken stories, finding near-duplicate articles, splitting fairly, and turning text, frames and speech into numbers.

Words to know

  • Preprocessing: cleaning and preparing data before training.
  • Near-duplicate: two articles that are almost the same story, for example the same article published twice with small edits.
  • 8-word phrases (shingles): we cut each article into overlapping pieces of 8 words, so two articles can be compared piece by piece.
  • Jaccard similarity: shared pieces divided by all pieces. If two articles share more than 30% of their 8-word phrases, we treat them as the same story.
  • Data leakage: when test stories (or copies of them) sneak into training. It makes scores look better than they really are.
  • Vocabulary: the list of words the model knows. Ours has 19,531 words that appear at least twice in training and have a GloVe vector.
  • Greedy ROUGE labels: for CNN, which has no official labels, we pick sentences one at a time, each time adding the one that raises ROUGE the most.

What to say

Before training, we prepared the data in nine steps. We removed 33 stories with no text and 80 with empty or broken videos, leaving 2,144. We searched for near-duplicate articles by comparing 8-word phrases and found 892 duplicate pairs. We split the data using the official test list, cleaned the text, labelled the key sentences, turned frames into numbers with ResNet-50, turned speech into timed sentences with Whisper, and turned every sentence into numbers with RoBERTa.

If the jury asks

  • Why remove duplicates? If the same story is in both training and test, the model has effectively seen the answer. We built a duplicate-free split to check for this. Our main results use the official split so they can be compared with the published papers.
  • What happens to speech with no words? Whisper sometimes invents words over music or silence. We drop low-confidence speech, and keep a story's speech only if at least 5 real words remain (1,207 stories).
  • Where can I download the data? github.com/xiyan-fu/MM-AVS, the official page. It links to the authors' OneDrive.

How M2SM reads an article and its video

Research paper 1 · M2SM (Fu et al., 2020)

The big idea

The architecture of M2SM, the first paper. It turns three inputs into numbers, reads each in order with BiLSTMs, connects them with attention, combines opinions with late+ fusion, and gives two outputs: a score for every sentence and the key frames. The diagram builds in five steps.

Words to know

  • GloVe: a ready-made 'dictionary' where each word is a list of 100 numbers, and words with similar meanings get similar numbers.
  • LSTM: a network that reads a sequence one item at a time and remembers what came before.
  • BiLSTM: two LSTMs: one reads forwards, one backwards. So every item knows what comes before and after it.
  • Word BiLSTM / sentence BiLSTM: the first reads the words of one sentence and makes one vector for the sentence. The second reads the sentences of the article in order.
  • Attention: a way for one item to decide which other items to focus on, by giving every pair a match score.
  • Bi-hop attention: a sentence reaches the video in two hops: sentence → transcript → frame. The transcript is the bridge, because it is words (like the article) and tied to the video's timeline.
  • Late+ fusion: combines the text's opinion and the video's opinion about each sentence, turning each down a bit when the other is already confident.
  • Cross-entropy loss: the training signal for sentences: it measures how wrong the model's yes/no guess was.
  • Reinforcement learning: learning by trial and error with a reward, used for picking frames because there are no frame labels.

What to say

This is M2SM. Step one: everything becomes numbers, words through GloVe and video frames through ResNet-50. Step two: BiLSTMs read each sequence forwards and backwards. Step three: attention connects the sources, and the transcript is the bridge between text and video. Step four: late+ fusion combines the text and video opinions. Step five: we get a score for every sentence, trained with cross-entropy, and key frames, trained with reinforcement learning.

If the jury asks

  • Why use the transcript as a bridge? Text and pictures are hard to compare directly. The transcript is text, so it matches the article, and it belongs to the video's timeline, so it matches the frames.
  • What is 'bi-stream'? Training the sentence part and the frame part together, so each can help the other.
  • How many parameters? About 3.5 million in our version.

Words to know

  • Score 0 to 1: how important the model thinks a sentence is. 1 = definitely key.
  • Epoch: one full pass over all training stories.
  • Training loss: how wrong the model is on the training data. Lower is better.
  • Overfitting: when a model memorises the training data instead of learning general patterns: training loss keeps going down but the validation score gets worse.
  • −ln (negative natural log): the maths behind cross-entropy. A confident right answer gives a small number; a confident wrong answer gives a big one.
  • Reward: the score the reinforcement learner tries to increase. Here: how varied and representative the chosen frames are.

  • How did you choose epoch 3? It had the best average of ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L on the validation set. We never look at the test set to choose.

Three ideas we implemented line by line

M2SM · the key equations, in plain words

Bilinear attention

score = v · (q ⊙ k + q + k)

A sentence (q) and a frame (k) are compared both by multiplying and by adding, so the model sees how strongly they match.

Late+ fusion

text × (1 − video)β

Each source's score is scaled down when the other source is confident, so one source can't drown out the other. β = 0.3.

Video reward

R = Rdiversity + Rrepresentative

Chosen frames should differ from each other and stand for the whole video.

The big idea

The three key formulas of M2SM, explained in words. You don't need to derive them; you need to say what each one does.

Words to know

  • q and k: two lists of numbers being compared: for example q is a sentence and k is a frame.
  • ⊙ (element-wise multiply): multiply the two lists position by position. High when both are strong in the same places, so it measures agreement.
  • β (beta): a small setting (0.3) that controls how strongly late+ fusion turns one source down when the other is confident.
  • Diversity reward: high when the chosen frames are different from each other.
  • Representativeness reward: high when the chosen frames cover the whole video well.

What to say

These are the three ideas we implemented line by line. Bilinear attention scores a sentence and a frame both by multiplying them, to see if they agree, and by adding them, to see how important each one is. Late+ fusion turns each source down a little when the other is already confident, so neither dominates. And the video reward makes chosen frames varied and representative of the whole video.

If the jury asks

  • Why multiply and add? Multiplying captures whether the two match; adding captures how important each one is on its own. Using both is what the paper calls dual interaction.
  • Did late+ fusion help? In our hybrid, switching it off changed nothing (−0.01 ROUGE-1). We say that honestly.

A newer design: one transformer for everything

Research paper 2 · A2Summ (He et al., CVPR 2023)

Unified transformer

Sentences and frames become one sequence. Attention lets every item look at every other item at once, instead of reading in order.

Shared attention, separate experts

One attention layer for all, but a separate feed-forward network for text and for video ("mixture of modality experts").

Contrastive learning

Matching text and video are pulled together; key and non-key items are pushed apart, both across stories and within one story.

Time alignment

Text may only attend to video from the same moment. But news articles have no timestamps, so A2Summ couldn't use this on Daily Mail.

The big idea

The second paper, A2Summ (CVPR 2023). Four ideas: one transformer for text and video together, shared attention with separate expert layers, contrastive learning, and time alignment, which it could not use on news articles because articles have no timestamps.

Words to know

  • Transformer: a model where every item can look at every other item at the same time, through attention. It's the architecture behind ChatGPT.
  • Token: one item in the transformer's input: here, one sentence or one video frame.
  • Feed-forward network (FFN): after attention, each item 'thinks alone': its numbers are expanded, filtered and shrunk back.
  • Mixture of modality experts: a separate FFN for each type of input (text, video), so each can specialise.
  • Contrastive learning: training by comparison: matching pairs (a story's text and its own video) are pulled together; non-matching ones are pushed apart.
  • Time alignment: text may only look at video from the same moment.

What to say

A2Summ is a newer design. It puts sentences and frames into one transformer, where everything can look at everything at once. Attention is shared, but text and video each get their own expert layer. It uses contrastive learning to make matching text and video agree. And it has time alignment, but news articles have no timestamps, so A2Summ couldn't use it on our dataset. That's the gap we filled.

If the jury asks

  • What is a CVPR paper? CVPR is one of the top computer-vision conferences. It's a strong, recent reference.
  • Why is a transformer better than a BiLSTM? Information can travel between any two items in one step instead of passing along a chain, and it trains well on modern hardware. Order has to be added with position markers.

Three things we noticed

Reading A2Summ's code carefully

Model picked on the test set

Their training script chose the best checkpoint by scoring the test set every epoch. Scores chosen this way look better than they are.

Summary length from the answer

At test time, each summary's length was set to the number of correct sentences, which a real system can't know.

Two layers were one

Their "2 layers" reuse the same layer twice (a Python list quirk). We used two real layers.

We report A2Summ's 44.11 ROUGE-1 for reference, but don't compare our numbers with it directly.

The big idea

We read A2Summ's public code and found three details that make its reported score look better than a strict test would. This shows we check evidence rather than trusting numbers. Present it respectfully: these are common shortcuts, not misconduct.

Words to know

  • Checkpoint: a saved copy of the model at one point during training.
  • Checkpoint selection on the test set: keeping the version that scored best on the test set. It makes the test score optimistic, because the test helped choose the model.
  • Early stopping: stopping training when the validation score stops improving. Good practice, as long as the validation set (not the test set) is used.
  • Summary length: how many sentences to pick. A real system must choose this without seeing the answer.

What to say

Reading A2Summ's code, we noticed three things. First, the best model was chosen by scoring the test set every epoch. Second, each summary's length was set to the number of correct sentences, which a real system can't know. Third, their two layers were actually one layer used twice, because of a Python detail. So we report their 44.11 for reference, but our evaluation is stricter and we don't compare with it directly.

If the jury asks

  • How do you know? Their training script on GitHub scores the test split every epoch to save the best model, and their evaluation uses the number of correct labels as the summary length.
  • How much does the length hint matter? Our hybrid scores 40.05 ROUGE-1 our way and 41.66 with their length rule, so about 1.6 points come from the hint alone.

The best of both papers, plus speech with timestamps

Our hybrid model

From M2SMFrom A2SummOur addition

The big idea

Our own model. It combines parts of M2SM (violet), parts of A2Summ (green) and our own additions (orange). The transformer is drawn as two stacked layers; the second layer is what lets the article receive video information through speech.

Words to know

  • RoBERTa: a large pretrained transformer that turns a sentence into 768 numbers. We use it as-is (frozen).
  • Transfer learning: reusing a model someone else already trained on huge data, instead of training from scratch.
  • Time-alignment mask: a table of allowed and blocked connections inside attention: a spoken sentence may only look at frames from its own time.
  • Focal loss: a version of cross-entropy that pays extra attention to hard examples.
  • Two stacked layers: the transformer's attention-then-experts step happens twice; layer 1's output is layer 2's input.
  • Parameters: the numbers a model learns. Our hybrid has about 6 million.

What to say

This is our hybrid. Article sentences become numbers with RoBERTa, and a BiLSTM from M2SM keeps their order. Frames come from ResNet-50, and speech comes from Whisper with timestamps, which is our addition. Everything goes into a two-layer transformer from A2Summ, with shared attention and a separate expert for text, video and speech. Inside attention is our time-alignment mask. Two layers make the bridge work: in layer 1 speech takes in its frames, and in layer 2 the article reads that speech. At the end, late+ fusion from M2SM scores the sentences, and a frame head picks key frames. It's trained with focal loss and contrastive losses, about 6 million parameters, on a laptop CPU.

If the jury asks

  • What exactly is new here? Combining the two papers, adding speech as a third input, and bringing A2Summ's time alignment to news through Whisper's timestamps.
  • Why keep the BiLSTM? Sentence order matters in news. Our ablation showed the transformer alone did about as well, which we report honestly.
  • How long did training take? About 30 to 50 seconds per epoch on the laptop CPU. Each run stopped automatically after 17 to 35 epochs, when the validation score had not improved for 10 epochs.

The big idea

An animation of our time-alignment idea. Each blue bar is a spoken sentence placed at its time; the frames from the same moment light up. Those are the only frames it is allowed to attend to.

Words to know

  • Timestamp: the start and end time of a spoken sentence, given by Whisper.
  • Attention mask: a rule that blocks some connections in attention by forcing their score to zero.

What to say

Whisper tells us when each sentence was spoken. We turned that into a rule: a spoken sentence may only look at frames from the same moment, with one second of slack. A2Summ needed timestamped text, and news articles don't have any. Speech provides the times, which brings A2Summ's idea to news. We checked it works: changing one spoken sentence from 0 to 7.5 seconds only changed frames 0 to 8, nothing else.

If the jury asks

  • Did it improve the results? Not measurably: switching it off changed ROUGE-1 by +0.10, which is within noise. The mechanism works, but on this data speech didn't help pick article sentences.
  • What if speech runs past the end of the video? That sentence is connected to the nearest frame instead.

Re-sample the 387 test stories 10,000 times to tell real gains from luck; switch parts off one at a time.

The big idea

The four tools we used to judge results fairly: ROUGE for summaries, Cos for frames, simple baselines as yardsticks, and statistics plus ablations to tell real gains from luck.

Words to know

  • ROUGE-1 / ROUGE-2 / ROUGE-L: shared single words, shared word pairs, and the longest run of words in the same order, between our summary and the journalist's. Higher is better.
  • F1: a balance of precision (how much of our summary is relevant) and recall (how much of the reference we covered). ROUGE scores here are F1.
  • Cos (cosine similarity): how similar two lists of numbers point. We compare our chosen frames with the photos the newspaper published.
  • Baseline: a simple method used as a yardstick, such as lead3 or random frames.
  • Paired bootstrap: re-sample the 387 test stories 10,000 times and recompute the difference each time. If the difference stays on one side of zero 95% of the time, it is real, not luck.
  • 95% interval: the range the true difference most likely lies in.
  • Ablation: switching one part off to see how much it contributes.

What to say

We judged everything with four tools. ROUGE counts how many words our summary shares with the journalist's. Cos measures how close our chosen frames are to the newspaper's photos. Baselines are simple methods every model must beat. And the paired bootstrap tells us whether a difference is real: we re-sample the test stories 10,000 times, and if the gain survives, it counts.

If the jury asks

  • Isn't ROUGE too simple? Yes, it only counts words, not meaning. That's why we mention human ratings and meaning-based scores as next steps.
  • Why 10,000 re-samples? It's a standard number that gives stable intervals and is cheap to compute.

The big idea

The abstractive extension. BART-large-CNN is a pretrained model that writes summaries word by word. Its summaries are as short as the journalist's (60 words vs 62), but score a little lower on ROUGE than lead3, partly because shorter text shares fewer words.

Words to know

  • BART: an encoder–decoder transformer by Facebook AI, trained on CNN/Daily Mail highlights.
  • Encoder: the part that reads and understands the article.
  • Decoder: the part that writes the summary one word at a time.
  • Greedy decoding: always pick the most likely next word. We chose it over beam search on validation.
  • Beam search: keep several candidate sentences at each step and choose the best overall. Slower.
  • Novel word pairs: pairs of words in the summary that never appear in the article. Only 18% for BART, so it mostly recombines the article's phrases.

What to say

Finally, an abstractive model. BART reads the whole article and writes a new summary word by word. We used it without retraining. Its summaries are about 60 words, almost exactly the journalist's 62, while lead3 uses 74. Because ROUGE counts shared words, the shorter BART summary scores a little lower on ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2, and the same on ROUGE-L. 82% of its word pairs come straight from the article, which is partly why it rarely invents facts.

If the jury asks

  • Is BART better or worse than your model? On ROUGE-1 it isn't significantly different from our hybrid. To a human reader it often looks better, which shows why ROUGE isn't the whole story.
  • Can it make mistakes? Yes, abstractive models can state things the article doesn't. That's why the app labels the AI summary and keeps the extractive one next to it.
  • Was BART trained on your test stories? BART's training articles are from about 2007–2015; our test stories cover 2018–19 events, so overlap is unlikely.
